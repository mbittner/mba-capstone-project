# 03_Preparation_Work — Summary

## Scope

This summary covers all 13 files in `03_Preparation_Work`: 11 PowerPoint preparation decks and two supporting PDF lecture decks. The material forms a guided introduction to choosing, designing, organising, and completing the MBA capstone dissertation.

Most of the core advice is still useful, but several slides are explicitly dated 2021 or 2022. The current Module Handbook, live RKC/YSJU systems, supervisor guidance, and dates assigned at enrolment must take precedence.

## Executive summary

The preparation material presents the dissertation as a focused investigation rather than a business change project or an attempt to prove a predetermined position. The project should begin with an area of genuine interest, then narrow to one answerable research question and a small set of realistic objectives. Topic choice must also fit the degree, available evidence, participant access, time, resources, and the researcher's capabilities.

The research question, objectives, literature review, methodology, findings, analysis, conclusions, recommendations, and reflection should form one connected argument. The literature review must critically evaluate recent and relevant research rather than merely describe sources. The research design must explain what will be investigated, why, from whom or what evidence, how, where, and when.

Planning is central. The decks describe a 30-week module, with a 2,000-word proposal due after six weeks and a 12,000-word dissertation at the end. These figures are historical guidance and must be confirmed for the applicable cohort. Ethics approval is required before any participant data is gathered.

## Key guidance

### 1. Dissertation purpose and topic fit

The dissertation requires the student to:

- investigate a topic related to the degree;
- gather and analyse information;
- interpret the results using relevant academic theory; and
- present the work as a coherent research report.

For Leading Innovation and Change, the decks identify leadership, innovation, change, strategy, and culture as relevant broad areas. Other programme variants have related subject lists.

A suitable topic should:

- be genuinely interesting enough to sustain several months of work;
- meet the programme's subject requirements;
- be researchable within the available time;
- have a credible body of academic literature;
- provide realistic access to data, documents, organisations, or participants; and
- match the researcher's resources, experience, and methodological skills.

The project should investigate and evaluate fairly. It should not begin with a conclusion that the research is intended to prove. It is also not, by itself, an implementation or organisational change project, although it may study a change initiative.

### 2. From a broad topic to a research question

The recommended progression is:

> Broad area of interest → particular focus → clear research question → specific objectives

The research question states what the project will find out. The objectives break that mission into the concrete areas to be researched and what will be done with the evidence.

The research-question lecture recommends **two to four objectives**. Each objective should be important, achievable, supported by accessible evidence, and directly connected to answering the research question.

An example in the material narrows “partnership working” to a comparison of how voluntary-sector and public-healthcare members perceive a particular partnership. Its objectives then cover:

1. reviewing relevant partnership literature;
2. gathering both groups' perceptions;
3. comparing the evidence and relating it to the literature; and
4. recommending actions based on the analysis.

Useful tests for a draft question:

- Does it identify the specific phenomenon, context, population, organisation, location, or time period?
- Can it be answered with evidence that is realistically accessible?
- Is it narrow enough for the word limit and module period?
- Does it invite investigation rather than assume an answer?
- Do the objectives collectively answer the question without adding unrelated work?

The decks recommend repeatedly writing, reviewing, and refining the question and objectives as the literature, access, and feasibility become clearer.

### 3. Scope and sample size

The examples demonstrate that analytical quality is more important than collecting the largest possible volume of data.

Indicative examples include:

- interview-only studies using approximately 8–15 semi-structured interviews, commonly 40–60 minutes each;
- questionnaire-only studies with 75 or more usable responses;
- mixed-method studies combining a survey with approximately 4–10 interviews; and
- qualitative studies combining interviews with organisational documents.

These are examples from successful dissertations, not mandatory minimums. The appropriate sample depends on the question, population, design, access, analytical method, expected response rate, and current supervisor/module guidance. Mixed methods can provide complementary evidence but may also create more data than can be analysed properly.

### 4. Research design

The planning framework asks:

- **What** do you want to find out?
- **Why** is it worth finding out?
- **Who or what** will provide the evidence?
- **How** will the evidence be collected and analysed?
- **When and where** will the research take place?

#### Qualitative approach

Typically involves smaller samples, interviews or other detailed evidence, and thematic analysis. It is suited to exploring experiences, meanings, processes, and phenomena in depth.

#### Quantitative approach

Typically involves larger samples, questionnaires or numerical data, and statistical analysis. It is suited to measuring relationships, differences, frequencies, or common patterns.

#### Mixed methods

Combines qualitative and quantitative evidence. It should be used only when both forms of evidence make a clear contribution to the research question and can be collected and analysed within the available time.

#### Inductive strategy

Begins with a research question and empirical data, develops concepts or explanations from the data, and relates the emerging interpretation to published theory. The slides associate this mainly with qualitative research.

#### Deductive strategy

Begins with existing theory, derives hypotheses and measurable concepts, gathers evidence, and uses the results to confirm, reject, or amend the theoretical expectations. The slides associate this often, though not exclusively, with surveys and quantitative data.

Methods mentioned include interviews, questionnaires, focus groups, document analysis, observation/ethnography, and action research. The selected method must be justified in relation to the question, objectives, evidence required, limitations, ethics, and feasibility.

### 6. Dissertation coherence

The material repeatedly emphasises that a dissertation is not a collection of independent chapters. Its internal logic should be:

1. The introduction establishes the problem, question, and objectives.
2. The literature review critically examines the concepts, theories, debates, and evidence relevant to that question.
3. The methodology explains and justifies how the objectives will be addressed.
4. The findings present the evidence generated by the chosen methods.
5. The analysis interprets that evidence in relation to the research question and literature.
6. The conclusions answer the question and objectives.
7. Recommendations follow from the evidence and analysis.
8. The reflection evaluates the research process, decisions, limitations, and learning.

Planning this alignment early and revising the whole dissertation near the end are presented as characteristics of strong work.

### 7. Literature review and searching

A literature review should establish:

- what is already known;
- which concepts and theories are relevant;
- which research methods have previously been used;
- major debates or controversies;
- inconsistencies between published findings; and
- gaps or unanswered questions.

A strong review uses recent and relevant work, questions assumptions and unsupported claims, compares authors, evaluates evidence and conclusions, and explains how each source contributes to the project. A weak review relies on old or general textbooks, merely describes sources, or claims that literature is unavailable without conducting a thorough search.

#### Search workflow

1. Clarify the research question.
2. Identify its main concepts.
3. List keywords, synonyms, spelling variants, and related terms for each concept.
4. Combine different concepts with `AND`.
5. Combine synonyms with `OR`.
6. Use quotation marks for exact phrases.
7. Use truncation, such as `leader*`, to retrieve variants including leader, leaders, and leadership.
8. Apply justified limits such as publication date, peer-reviewed status, document type, or abstract search.
9. Review the results and refine the terms iteratively.
10. Record the final searches so the process can be explained and repeated.

The 2022-era deck lists Discover/EBSCO, Emerald, Business Source Premier, Scopus, PsycINFO/PsycArticles, Global Newsstream, Euromonitor, HS Talks, FAME, MarketLine, Global Market Navigator, and Mintel. Current availability and access links should be checked through the live YSJ Library site.

Google Scholar is recommended as a supplementary tool, particularly for citation trails. The slides recommend configuring York St John library links and using advanced search. Database searching should remain the main structured approach.

The material recommends:

- saving searches and results in database accounts;
- using a reference manager for citations and the Harvard-style bibliography;
- recording sources and search strategies separately; and
- maintaining a literature matrix, for example in Excel, categorised by theme, purpose, method, findings, and conclusions.

The slides refer to RefWorks. Confirm which reference-management tool and Harvard variant are currently supported or required.

### 8. Working with the supervisor

Before the module begins, the student completes a dissertation proposal questionnaire. RKC uses it when approaching a potential supervisor, who then makes initial contact if they accept the allocation.

At the outset, agree:

- communication channels and frequency;
- expectations for plans and draft submissions;
- lead time needed for feedback;
- support for interview guides or questionnaires;
- help interpreting qualitative or quantitative analysis; and
- milestones for proposal and dissertation drafts.

The student remains responsible for research decisions. The supervisor provides advice, challenge, and feedback and is described as the dissertation's first marker. Feedback should be requested with sufficient notice, read carefully, and acted upon.

If the relationship is not working, first raise the concern with the supervisor. If it remains unresolved, contact RKC student services.

### 10. Research ethics

All projects must comply with the University's research ethics requirements. Participants must:

- understand the study and what participation involves;
- freely consent;
- not be exposed to harm;
- have their confidentiality and anonymity protected; and
- have their data stored securely with restricted access.

The decks describe a block-approval route for eligible MBA projects. The block route excludes research involving:

- offensive or potentially illegal primary material;
- terrorist or extremist groups; and
- sensitive subjects including political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, health data, sex life, sexual orientation, and criminal convictions or offences.

Applications are submitted through the RKC Thesis Repository and reviewed by the supervisor and an RKC panel. Participant information and consent materials should be shown to the supervisor before use.

Research falling outside the block criteria requires early discussion with the supervisor and a more detailed approval process involving YSJU. If the project changes, a second ethics application may be required.

**No participant recruitment or data collection should begin until the applicable written ethics approval has been received.** Data collected prematurely may be unusable.

## Common risks highlighted in the material

- Choosing a question that is too broad.
- Treating implementation of a change as equivalent to research.
- Designing the study to prove a preferred answer.
- Relying only on literature from earlier modules.
- Describing sources rather than critically evaluating them.
- Assuming participants or organisational data will be accessible.
- Underestimating recruitment, pilot testing, data cleaning, transcription, or analysis time.
- Choosing mixed methods without enough time to analyse both datasets properly.
- Treating chapters as separate deliverables instead of one argument.
- Waiting too long to request supervisor feedback.
- Collecting data before ethics approval.
- Failing to resubmit for ethics review after a material change.
- Relying on historical dates, rules, links, or assessment criteria from these decks.

## Recommended next steps

### Immediate preparation

1. Confirm the current Module Handbook, assessment brief, marking criteria, word limits, ethics process, enrolment eligibility, and module start options.
2. Write down three possible topic areas linked to Leading Innovation and Change.
3. For each topic, assess interest, academic relevance, participant/data access, literature availability, ethics risk, time, and methodological capability.
4. Select the most feasible topic and draft several alternative research questions.
5. Reduce these to one focused question and two to four objectives.
6. Identify the population, organisation, documents, or dataset that can answer each objective.

### Literature preparation

1. Break the research question into two to four key concepts.
2. Build a search table with synonyms, spelling variants, exact phrases, and truncations.
3. Search the current YSJ business databases and Google Scholar.
4. Save and document the search strings, databases, filters, and dates.
5. Create a literature matrix covering citation, theory, context, method, sample, findings, limitations, themes, and relevance.
6. Use the current required reference manager and Harvard guidance.
7. Use the literature to refine the question, objectives, conceptual framing, and method.

### Research design and feasibility

1. Choose a provisional qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method design.
2. Explain why the design is the best fit for the research question.
3. Define a justified sample and realistic recruitment route.
4. Secure preliminary organisational/gatekeeper access without collecting research data.
5. Draft a data-collection instrument and plan a pilot or test.
6. Build a timeline covering proposal writing, ethics, access, piloting, recruitment, fieldwork, transcription/data cleaning, analysis, chapter drafting, feedback, revision, and contingency.

### Supervision and ethics

1. Complete the current proposal questionnaire by the official deadline.
2. Agree a working protocol and milestone plan with the supervisor.
3. Discuss any ethics uncertainty immediately.
4. Prepare participant information, consent, gatekeeper, and data-collection materials.
5. Submit the ethics application early enough to absorb review or revision.
6. Wait for written approval before recruiting participants or gathering data.

### Writing and quality control

1. Map every proposed chapter and section to the research question, objectives, and marking criteria.
2. Draft the literature review and methodology early.
3. Reserve substantial time for analysis rather than only data collection.
4. Use supervisor feedback at agreed milestones.
5. Review the completed dissertation for alignment, criticality, evidence, coherence, limitations, and consistency of conclusions and recommendations.
