# Effective humanitarian leadership in Sudan

**Dissertation:** *Effective Humanitarian Leadership in Sudan: Beyond conventional paths, behaviours and competencies*  
**Author:** Carine Jonckheere (2014)  
**Best for:** An advanced qualitative study linking a difficult operating context to leadership behaviours and competencies.

## Research question

> What, in practice, are the principal competencies and skills needed for a humanitarian leader to be effective in the Sudanese context?

## Objectives

The dissertation aims to:

1. Critically review leadership in context.
2. Evaluate contextual factors and determine the strength of the Sudanese humanitarian environment.
3. Identify and analyse behaviours and coping mechanisms used by effective leaders.
4. Connect those behaviours to leadership skills and competencies identified in the literature.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

This is an exploratory, inductive, qualitative, multi-case study guided by a pragmatic applied philosophy.

- The core leader sample comprised eight international NGO country directors and two heads of programmes.
- Contextual evidence was triangulated through interviews with four representatives of multilateral or bilateral donors and through organisational and sector documents.
- Humanitarian leaders were interviewed twice: first about contextual conditions, then through semi-structured behavioural-event and intent questions.
- Donors were interviewed about the wider operating environment.
- Three pilot interviews informed the process and were excluded from the final dataset.
- Interviews lasted about one hour and were conducted between March and May 2014.
- Documents included donor reports, situation reports, cluster and security minutes, agreements, and policies.
- Analysis occurred in two phases: contextual evidence was mapped against a “4Cs” situational-strength model, then leadership behaviours and mechanisms were coded and compared.

### Key literature and conceptual framework

The study integrates:

- **Leadership in context:** leaders, followers, organisations, and external environments interact.
- **Situational and contingency leadership:** effectiveness depends on matching behaviour to circumstances.
- **Flexible, versatile, and strategic leadership:** leaders must change approach while keeping long-term direction.
- **Complexity leadership:** useful where authority is distributed and outcomes emerge from networks.
- **Situational strength:** the 4Cs—clarity, consistency, constraints, and consequences—are used to assess how strongly context limits behaviour.
- **Authentic and ethical leadership:** values, purpose, and integrity matter when compromise is constantly required.
- **Contextual, cultural, social, and emotional intelligence:** leaders need to read people, institutions, and political signals.
- **Humanitarian principles and leadership:** neutrality, independence, humanity, and impartiality can conflict with operational realities.
- **Bricolage and adaptation:** combining limited resources and working within restrictions.
- **Innovation:** differentiating the organisation, building coalitions, and creating locally appropriate alternatives.
- **Remote leadership:** leading dispersed or difficult-to-access teams through trust, clarity, and presence at a distance.

## Main findings

Sudan was characterised as a strong situation:

- requirements were often clear but inconsistent across government, donors, and organisational actors;
- constraints relating to regulation, funding, security, access, and human resources were high;
- consequences for non-compliance could be substantial.

Two broadly successful leadership patterns emerged:

1. **Adaptive or bricolage-like leadership:** fitting donor and national requirements, growing portfolios incrementally, networking extensively, and using flexible conformity to keep programmes operating.
2. **Innovative leadership:** developing specialist niches, building bottom-up coalitions, differentiating the organisation, encouraging local ownership, and acting from a clearer sense of authentic purpose.

Across both patterns, effectiveness depended on:

- contextual intelligence and willingness to learn;
- cultural, social, emotional, and interpersonal skill;
- diplomacy, trust, and behavioural flexibility;
- strategic and creative thinking;
- mastery of both emergency relief and longer-term development work;
- assertive, purposeful leadership of remote teams;
- the capacity to balance organisational survival, stakeholder demands, and humanitarian ethics.

The study also identifies an ethical risk. Highly adaptive leaders may gradually accept compromises and become cynical, while innovative leaders reported a stronger sense of authenticity and fulfilment.

Seven propositions connect effectiveness to contextual intelligence, continual learning, trustworthy diplomacy, strategic creativity, dual relief/development expertise, context-bound innovation, and purposeful team leadership.

## Conclusion and recommendations

Effective humanitarian leadership in Sudan cannot be reduced to a universal competency checklist. Competence is expressed through behaviour adapted to a strong and contradictory context.

The dissertation recommends:

- contextualising recruitment, assessment, training, and performance evaluation;
- selecting leaders for learning ability and contextual intelligence, not only technical expertise;
- giving country leaders sufficient decision latitude;
- providing stronger head-office support and a dedicated support function;
- creating an organisational culture where dilemmas and failures can be discussed openly;
- assessing whether headquarters and donors are genuinely prepared to support innovation;
- continuing to examine how humanitarian principles are preserved under operational pressure.

## Limitations and cautions

- The purposive sample is small and concentrated in one national context.
- Effectiveness is inferred mainly from participant and donor accounts rather than programme outcomes.
- The researcher interprets complex political and ethical behaviour, so reflexivity is important.
- The two leadership patterns are analytical categories, not rigid leader types.

## Why read this dissertation

Read this for a sophisticated example of context-first leadership research. It is particularly valuable for humanitarian work, international NGOs, institutional complexity, behavioural-event interviewing, multi-level triangulation, and building propositions from qualitative data.
