# MBA Dissertation — Big-Picture Guide

## Purpose of this guide

This document brings together the summaries in `01_Pre_Residency` through `08_Friday`. ## The dissertation in one view

The dissertation is an independent, theory-informed investigation into a focused business or management question related to the degree. It is not simply a literature essay, a consultancy report, or an organisational change project.

The overall logic is:

> Topic and access → literature search → research question and objectives → research design → proposal → ethics approval → data collection → analysis → dissertation → reflection and revision → submission

Every stage constrains the next one. A broad or unanswerable question produces a weak design. A method chosen before access is checked may be infeasible. Data collected without ethics approval may be unusable. Findings that are not analysed through relevant literature cannot support strong conclusions.

The strongest dissertations maintain one connected line of reasoning:

1. The **research question** states what will be found out.
2. The **objectives** define the work required to answer it.
3. The **literature review** establishes relevant knowledge, theory, debates, methods, and gaps.
4. The **methodology** explains how appropriate evidence will be gathered and analysed.
5. The **findings** present that evidence.
6. The **analysis/discussion** interprets the findings through the literature.
7. The **conclusions** answer the question and objectives.
8. The **recommendations** follow from the evidence and remain realistic for the context.
9. The **reflection** critically evaluates the research process and the researcher's development.

## Historical assessment framework

The 2021–2022 material describes a 60-credit capstone module with two submissions:

| Submission | Historical length | Module weighting |
|---|---:|---:|
| Research proposal | 2,000 words, with a stated 10% allowance | 20% |
| Dissertation | 12,000 words, with a stated 10% allowance | 80% |

The historical proposal criteria are:

| Proposal component | Weight within proposal | Indicative words |
|---|---:|---:|
| Problem definition and research question/objectives | 15% | 300 |
| Literature review | 60% | 1,200 |
| Methodology and research design | 20% | 400 |
| Limitations and potential bias | 5% | 100 |
| Timeline/Gantt chart | No stated mark | Not specified |

The historical dissertation criteria are:

| Dissertation component | Weight within dissertation |
|---|---:|
| Abstract and introduction | 10% |
| Literature review and methodology | 40% |
| Findings, analysis, conclusions, and recommendations | 40% |
| Reflection on the dissertation process | 10% |

The supplied materials describe an overall pass mark of 50%, limited compensation between the proposal and dissertation, and one resubmission opportunity for failed work. They also state that proposal material may be reused directly in the dissertation because it belongs to the same module. Material from unrelated earlier assignments remains subject to normal quotation, citation, and academic-integrity requirements.

These are historical rules, not a substitute for the current assessment brief.

## Stage-by-stage process

### 1. Confirm the current rules and prepare for the Residency

Before making irreversible project decisions:

- obtain the current Module Handbook and assessment briefs;
- confirm eligibility, module start options, deadlines, word-count rules, and referencing requirements;
- confirm the current ethics route and forms;
- review the live Online Campus and Thesis Repository;
- verify supervisor-contact expectations; and
- confirm current library databases, survey tools, and support contacts.

For an online Residency, install and test the required meeting software, microphone, webcam, headphones, and internet connection. Check the official session timezone and join early enough to resolve technical issues.

Detailed guidance: [Pre-Residency summary](01_Pre_Residency/summary.md)

### 2. Select a feasible topic

A suitable topic sits at the intersection of:

- genuine interest;
- degree relevance;
- credible academic literature;
- access to participants, documents, organisations, or datasets;
- manageable ethics risk;
- available time and resources; and
- the researcher's methodological skills.

For Leading Innovation and Change, the historical materials identify leadership, innovation, change, strategy, and culture as relevant broad areas.

Begin with several possible topics, then test each against feasibility. Do not commit simply because a topic is important or interesting. A worthwhile question that cannot be evidenced within the module period is not a workable dissertation project.

The dissertation investigates a phenomenon. It may study a change initiative, but carrying out a change is not itself the research. The project should also remain open to unexpected findings rather than attempting to prove a preferred position.

### 3. Search the literature and narrow the question

Move from:

> Broad interest → specific issue and context → one research question → three or four objectives

The recommended question is focused, answerable, neutral, and bounded by the relevant organisation, population, sector, geography, event, or time period.

The historical guidance recommends:

- **one research question**;
- **no separate layer of research aims**; and
- **three or four objectives**.

The first objective should normally identify the literature to be critically reviewed. The remaining objectives should explain what evidence will be gathered and how it will be analysed. A recommendation objective should be included only when the design can support recommendations of the intended scope.

Conduct a structured literature search:

1. Break the question into key concepts.
2. Identify keywords, synonyms, phrase variants, and spelling variants.
3. Combine concepts with `AND` and synonyms with `OR`.
4. Use exact-phrase searching and truncation where appropriate.
5. Search current YSJ business databases and supplement with Google Scholar.
6. Apply justified date, peer-review, document-type, and field filters.
7. Record databases, dates, search strings, filters, and results.
8. Follow citation trails to relevant earlier and later publications.

Create two separate records:

- a **reference library** for citation management; and
- a **literature matrix** recording theory, context, method, sample, findings, limitations, themes, and relevance.

A strong review evaluates and synthesises literature. It does not merely summarize one source after another. It should identify what is known, relevant theories and concepts, methodological patterns, debates, inconsistent results, and unanswered questions.

Detailed guidance: [Preparation Work summary](03_Preparation_Work/summary.md) and [Monday summary](04_Monday/summary.md)

### 4. Choose the research strategy

The method must follow the question and the evidence needed. It should not be selected only because it is familiar or because a software tool is available.

Ask:

- What exactly must be found out?
- Why does it matter?
- Who or what can provide credible evidence?
- How will that evidence be gathered?
- How will it be analysed?
- When and where will the research occur?
- What claims will the resulting data legitimately support?

#### Qualitative research

Usually uses a smaller sample and detailed evidence, often semi-structured interviews, focus groups, observation, or documents. It is suited to experiences, meanings, processes, perceptions, and exploration of selected cases.

Semi-structured interviews use a short list of core questions plus follow-up probes. Good interview practice requires clear and open questions, rapport, active listening, piloting, supervisor review, and consent for any recording.

The historical materials commonly illustrate interview-only studies with approximately 8–15 participants and interviews of roughly 40–60 minutes. These are examples, not automatic requirements.

#### Quantitative research

Usually gathers standardised data from a larger sample, commonly through questionnaires, and analyses patterns, differences, or associations statistically.

A questionnaire must be designed from the research question and relevant literature. Every item should have an analytical purpose. Questions and response options must be clear, non-overlapping, inclusive, and piloted.

The historical materials use approximately 75 usable responses as a rough questionnaire-only guideline, subject to population size and design. Invitations must exceed the desired response count because the response rate is uncertain.

#### Mixed methods

Mixed methods may provide complementary breadth and depth, but it increases design, ethics, recruitment, analysis, and writing work. It should be chosen only when both datasets make a necessary contribution that can be completed and integrated within the module period.

#### Other design choices

The project should also establish:

- **deductive or inductive reasoning:** testing theory-derived expectations versus developing an interpretation from evidence;
- **exploratory, descriptive, or explanatory purpose**;
- **cross-sectional or longitudinal timing**;
- **fixed or flexible collection procedures**; and
- the **unit of analysis**, such as person, team, organisation, event, document, or period.

Detailed guidance: [Tuesday summary](05_Tuesday/summary.md) and [Wednesday summary](06_Wednesday/summary.md)

### 5. Consider secondary data

Participant research is not the only option. Existing material may supplement primary evidence or form the main dataset.

Potential sources include:

- company reports, internal surveys, minutes, plans, and communications;
- public reports, news, documentaries, biographies, and archives;
- organisational, financial, industry, or public datasets;
- existing interview or monitoring records; and
- academic publications used in a systematic review or meta-analysis.

Secondary data must be evaluated for:

- fit with the research question;
- credibility and completeness;
- original purpose and likely bias;
- ownership and permission;
- confidentiality and lawful use;
- ethics implications; and
- whether the proposed analysis makes a genuinely new contribution.

Existing participant-derived data is not automatically exempt from ethics review.

### 6. Prepare and discuss the project concept

The Residency culminates in a short discussion of one provisional project idea. The historical format allows approximately 10 minutes to explain the idea and 10 minutes for tutor and peer feedback.

The Dissertation Development Form prompts the student to identify:

- the broad area and specific issue;
- accessible people, documents, or other evidence;
- relevant literature;
- a draft question; and
- draft objectives.

A useful concept presentation covers:

1. context and research problem;
2. question and objectives;
3. theoretical or literature base;
4. evidence and access;
5. strategy, sample, and method;
6. analysis plan;
7. ethics issues;
8. feasibility and limitations; and
9. the most important uncertainty requiring feedback.

The idea is not binding. Record feedback, test it against evidence and constraints, and revise the design deliberately.

Detailed guidance: [Thursday summary](07_Thursday/summary.md) and [Friday summary](08_Friday/summary.md)

### 7. Complete the proposal questionnaire and establish supervision

The historical RKC proposal questionnaire asks for:

- working title;
- principal aim;
- key terms;
- country or geographical area;
- sector, industry, or company;
- essential sources;
- research method; and
- other relevant details.

RKC uses this information to identify a suitable supervisor. Describe the proposed method precisely enough to reveal the expertise required. Use the additional-information field for specialist analysis, access constraints, language or geography, sensitive ethics issues, or other supervisory needs.

Once allocated, agree:

- communication channels and frequency;
- draft-feedback milestones;
- expected turnaround time;
- support for instruments and analysis;
- use of the Thesis Repository; and
- the latest practical date for substantive feedback before submission.

The student makes the research decisions. The supervisor advises, challenges, and comments. Historical documents refer to minimum interactions, but purposeful contact around key decisions and drafts is more important than simply meeting a number.

### 8. Write the proposal

The proposal must show that the project is focused, grounded in literature, feasible, ethical, and methodologically defensible.

It should include:

- the problem, significance, question, and objectives;
- a critical literature review;
- the research philosophy, strategy, sample, data source, collection instrument, and analysis plan;
- justification of the method against credible alternatives;
- limitations, bias, and mitigation;
- ethics considerations; and
- a realistic project schedule.

The historical rubric gives 60% of proposal marks to the literature review, making early and systematic searching essential.

Prepare participant documents, data-collection instruments, access arrangements, and the ethics submission alongside the proposal where current procedures permit. Do not confuse planning or piloting with authorised participant data collection.

### 9. Obtain ethics approval

This is a mandatory gate:

> Do not recruit participants or collect participant data until written ethics approval has been received.

The historical materials describe a short/block-approval route for lower-risk MBA research and a full route for higher-risk work. The block route excludes specified sensitive topics, vulnerable participants, extremist or illegal material, and other elevated risks.

Every participant study must address:

- informed and voluntary consent;
- participant welfare;
- withdrawal;
- confidentiality and anonymity;
- secure storage and restricted access;
- GDPR/data protection;
- recording and transcription;
- gatekeepers and power relationships; and
- retention and destruction of research data.

Participant information sheets, consent wording, gatekeeper materials, interview guides, and questionnaires should be reviewed by the supervisor before use. A significant change to participants, method, questions, or data may require renewed approval.

Detailed forms and templates: [Key Documents summary](02_Key_Documents/summary.md)

### 10. Collect and analyse the evidence

Pilot the instrument and technology before main collection. Track recruitment, consent, response rates, deviations from the approved plan, and data quality.

For qualitative data:

1. become familiar with recordings, notes, transcripts, or documents;
2. generate defensible codes;
3. combine related codes into themes;
4. review themes against extracts and the whole dataset;
5. define and name themes consistently; and
6. select evidence and relate the analysis to the question and literature.

Codes may be deductively derived from theory, inductively developed from the evidence, or produced through a transparent hybrid approach. The dissertation should include quotations or extracts so readers can assess the interpretation.

For quantitative data:

- clean and document the dataset;
- select statistics suited to the variables and assumptions;
- distinguish mean, median, and mode appropriately;
- use inferential tests only when justified;
- report sample and missing data transparently; and
- avoid treating correlation as proof of causation.

Software does not repair weak research design. Straightforward analysis may be possible in Excel, while more advanced work may need specialist software and supervision.

### 11. Write, integrate, reflect, and revise

Write chapter by chapter and seek feedback at agreed milestones, but reserve time to revise the complete dissertation as one argument.

Useful alignment check:

| Element | Core question |
|---|---|
| Introduction | Is the problem, context, question, and purpose clear? |
| Literature review | Does it establish the concepts and evidence used by the study? |
| Methodology | Can the design answer every objective credibly and ethically? |
| Findings | Is the evidence presented clearly and transparently? |
| Analysis | Is the evidence interpreted through the relevant literature? |
| Conclusions | Is the research question explicitly answered? |
| Recommendations | Do they follow from the evidence and fit the context? |
| Reflection | Is there critical, evidence-based learning and a future action plan? |

Maintain a short reflective journal throughout the project. Record major decisions, assumptions, problems, feedback, changes, skills, and learning. Strong reflection minimises narrative, examines other perspectives, uses relevant literature, evaluates evidence of success or failure, and ends with a justified action plan.

## Critical dependencies

The following gates deserve particular attention:

1. **Current rules before planning:** historical guidance must be checked.
2. **Literature before finalising the question:** the project needs a credible theoretical and empirical base.
3. **Access before committing to the design:** participant or data access is a major feasibility risk.
4. **Question before instrument:** interviews and questionnaires must be designed to answer the question.
5. **Supervisor/ethics review before collection:** participant-facing material requires scrutiny.
6. **Written ethics approval before participant data:** no exceptions should be assumed.
7. **Analysis before conclusions:** recommendations cannot exceed the evidence.
8. **Whole-document revision before submission:** chapter quality alone does not guarantee coherence.

## Common failure modes

- Choosing a topic that is too broad.
- Creating many overlapping aims and objectives.
- Designing research to prove a desired conclusion.
- Using only literature from earlier taught modules.
- Describing literature rather than critically synthesising it.
- Assuming access to busy participants or confidential data.
- Choosing mixed methods because it appears more impressive.
- Writing survey questions that do not answer the research question.
- Using a small sample to make organisation-wide claims.
- Confusing frequency with qualitative importance.
- Treating secondary sources as neutral or automatically ethical.
- Collecting data before approval.
- Underestimating recruitment, piloting, transcription, cleaning, and analysis.
- Requesting supervisor feedback too close to the deadline.
- Writing chapters as unrelated assignments.
- Producing a reflective chronology without critical evaluation or future action.

- [ ] Verify eligibility, start date, and official deadlines.

- [ ] Agree working arrangements with the supervisor.
- [ ] Develop the critical literature review.
- [ ] Justify the sample, collection method, and analysis.
- [ ] Draft and pilot instruments where ethically permitted.
- [ ] Prepare ethics and participant-facing documents.
- [ ] Obtain feedback on the proposal draft.
- [ ] Submit the proposal by the official deadline.

### Before and during fieldwork

- [ ] Obtain written ethics approval.
- [ ] Confirm gatekeeper and data-owner permissions.
- [ ] Use approved participant information and consent processes.
- [ ] Store identifiers, raw data, and consent records securely.
- [ ] Document changes and seek renewed approval where required.
- [ ] Start writing the literature and methodology chapters.

### Analysis and writing

- [ ] Follow a documented analytical procedure.
- [ ] Preserve an audit trail from evidence to findings.
- [ ] Relate findings to the question, objectives, and literature.
- [ ] Make bounded conclusions and realistic recommendations.
- [ ] Obtain supervisor feedback early enough to act on it.
- [ ] Complete the critical reflection.
- [ ] Review the entire dissertation for alignment and coherence.
- [ ] Check referencing, anonymity, formatting, word count, and submission requirements.

## Final perspective

The dissertation is manageable when treated as a sequence of connected decisions rather than one large writing task. The most important early work is not producing pages—it is defining a question worth answering, confirming access to credible evidence, finding the literature that frames the issue, and choosing a design whose limitations are understood.

Once the project begins, progress depends on disciplined execution: obtain ethics approval, collect only the evidence the design requires, analyse it transparently, use supervisor feedback while there is time to act, and continually reconnect every chapter to the research question and objectives.
