## Main content

### Review of qualitative coding

The worked leadership case shows that coding identifies the meaning in specific evidence. A credible written analysis must:

- name the code or theme;
- provide extracts showing the evidence;
- justify the interpretation; and
- relate the finding to a relevant theoretical framework.

Examples from Dorothy's account are connected to transformational leadership:

- organisational purpose and a future warehouse vision → inspirational motivation;
- asking workers to investigate and design solutions → intellectual stimulation and participation;
- learning about individual skills and aspirations → individualised consideration.

Alternative frameworks, such as Kouzes and Posner's leadership practices, may also fit. There is no single inevitable coding scheme, but interpretations must be reasoned and evidenced. Sharing early coding with the supervisor is recommended.

The review repeats the thematic-analysis sequence: familiarisation, coding, theme development, theme review, definition/naming, and reporting.

### Research-purpose distinctions

The supporting note distinguishes:

- **Exploratory research:** clarifies a poorly understood issue; usually flexible and often qualitative.
- **Descriptive research:** develops an accurate account of what is happening.
- **Explanatory research:** investigates causal relationships and why events occur.

A dissertation can serve more than one purpose, but one is often dominant. Naming the purpose helps align the question, evidence, design, and strength of claims.

### Strategy choices

A complete research strategy should answer:

- What will be found out?
- Why does it matter?
- Who or what will provide evidence?
- How will the evidence be gathered?
- How will it be analysed and used?

Key design dimensions are:

#### Deductive or inductive

- A **deductive** design starts from theory, creates hypotheses and operational definitions, gathers evidence, and uses the results to confirm or amend theory. It often uses quantitative surveys.
- An **inductive** design starts from a question, gathers open evidence, develops an interpretation, and relates it to existing literature. It often uses qualitative interviews.

These are tendencies rather than rigid pairings.

#### Quantitative or qualitative

- Quantitative research aggregates numerical or categorised data and often seeks patterns that may represent a wider population.
- Qualitative research examines meaning, experience, language, or behaviour in depth and normally makes more bounded claims about selected cases.

#### Cross-sectional or longitudinal

- Cross-sectional research captures evidence at one point in time.
- Longitudinal research gathers evidence at multiple points to study change or development.

#### Fixed or flexible

- Fixed designs settle the main procedures before collection and are commonly quantitative.
- Flexible designs may evolve during collection and are commonly qualitative.
- A multi-strategy design may use a flexible exploratory stage followed by a fixed stage.

Methods mentioned include interviews, questionnaires, focus groups, documents, and observation. The chosen combination must be justified, not merely listed.

### Research-strategy exercise

Exercise 5 concerns virtual teamworking in a large bank during 2020–2021. The research question asks about strengths, weaknesses, and practices worth continuing. The objectives cover literature, employee and leader experiences, theory-linked analysis, and recommendations.

Two options are proposed:

- semi-structured interviews with six team leaders and six team members; or
- questionnaires distributed to more than 300 staff.

The task is to compare their strengths and limitations, select or adapt an approach, and justify it. Thursday's review notes the central trade-off:

- interviews offer depth, probing, and unexpected insights but weak population representativeness;
- questionnaires offer broader coverage and possible representativeness but constrain answers to researcher-defined categories and prevent probing.

Using both methods increases design, ethics, recruitment, analysis, and writing work. The method should follow the claims the project needs to make—not the desire to use more tools.

### Secondary and published data

Secondary data may supplement primary evidence or form the whole dissertation dataset. Possible sources include:

- company reports, press releases, surveys, minutes, plans, job descriptions, and internal communications;
- books, news reports, documentaries, biographies, and autobiographies;
- fictional works, if the research concerns representation rather than factual organisational reality;
- databases such as company or public-value datasets;
- existing recordings or organisational monitoring data; and
- collections of academic studies used in systematic review or meta-analysis.

The material stresses four tests:

1. Can the source answer the research question?
2. Why was it originally produced, selected, edited, or published?
3. How credible is it as evidence of the underlying reality?
4. Will the dissertation perform a new, theory-informed analysis rather than reproduce existing work?

Public-relations documents, autobiographies, and entertainment media require particular caution because they are selective and may be self-serving. Internal data requires permission from its owner. Previously collected information can still require ethics review.

Meta-analysis requires a sufficiently large literature, explicit and reproducible search criteria, inclusion/exclusion rules, and a defensible analytical procedure. It is not simply an expanded narrative review.

The historical session arrangement requires a Dissertation Development Form to be submitted no later than two hours after Thursday's session. The form is not assessed, but failure to submit historically meant no Friday discussion slot.

## Practical next steps

- Create a design-decision table comparing options by question fit, access, data quality, sample, ethics, analysis, time, skills, limitations, and claims supported.
- Write a one-paragraph justification for the preferred strategy and a one-paragraph limitations/mitigation statement.
- Verify permission and provenance for every proposed secondary source.
- Define the unit of analysis: person, team, organisation, event, document, period, or publication.
- Ensure the recommendation objective does not imply broader evidence than the design can provide.
- Prepare one concise project idea for Friday rather than several competing proposals.

## Important cautions

- Strategy labels differ between methods authors; define and reference the terminology used.
- A small diverse sample is not automatically representative of a large organisation.
- Mixed methods can double the workload without automatically improving validity.
- Secondary data is not ethics-free and must be assessed for ownership, consent, confidentiality, credibility, and lawful use.
- The Friday deadline and Zoom instructions are historical; confirm current session arrangements.
