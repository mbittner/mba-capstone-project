# Collaborative value creation in Canadian SME–non-profit partnerships

**Proposal:** *Perspectives on value creation in Canadian SME/Non-Profit CSR partnerships 2020: a maturity framework*  
**Author:** David Marshall (2020)  
**Best for:** Corporate social responsibility, cross-sector partnerships, shared value, impact measurement, and developing a practical maturity framework.

## Research problem and purpose

The proposal connects two trends in Canada:

- declining participation and trust in non-profit organisations, especially among younger donors; and
- increasing pressure on businesses to contribute positively to social justice, sustainability, and global challenges.

The author argues that these trends create an opportunity for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and non-profit organisations (NPOs) to move beyond philanthropy and transactional sponsorship. Deeper partnerships could combine complementary resources, build NPO capacity, strengthen business purpose and legitimacy, and create measurable economic, social, and environmental value.

The practical purpose is to develop a maturity framework that helps a Canadian NPO working on social-justice issues in the Global South establish more effective partnerships with Canadian SMEs.

## Research question

> How can a Canadian NPO engaged in social justice issues in the Global South and a Canadian SME effectively collaborate to create measurable shared value?

## Objectives

The proposed research aims to:

1. Critically evaluate literature on collaborative value creation between businesses and NPOs.
2. Use qualitative interviews to evaluate how Canadian SME–NPO collaborations can be optimised to create meaningful and measurable value for both parties.
3. Develop a maturity framework to guide partnerships between a focal Canadian NPO and Canadian SMEs.

## How the proposed study is built

### Research design

The proposal describes a cross-sectional, exploratory, qualitative study.

- **Philosophy:** interpretivism, with attention to how participants construct meaning through dialogue.
- **Approach:** inductive theory development.
- **Method:** semi-structured interviews treated as co-constructed conversations.
- **Sample:** ten CEOs of Canadian SMEs, selected purposively for their decision-making authority over CSR strategy.
- **Data capture:** recorded interviews, verbatim transcripts, written observations of reactions and body language, and secondary literature.
- **Analysis:** thematic content analysis, with categories and themes emerging from the data.
- **Triangulation:** emerging patterns will be compared with secondary literature.
- **Validation:** limited participant validation is proposed.
- **Researcher stance:** reflexivity is emphasised because the researcher’s social and ethical commitments may shape questions and interpretation.

The proposed participant group represents SME decision makers. The design does not clearly include equivalent interviews with NPO leaders, despite the bilateral research question.

## Key literature and conceptual framework

### Corporate social responsibility

The proposal traces CSR from philanthropy and voluntary responsibility toward a broader expectation that companies manage social, environmental, and economic effects in line with stakeholder expectations. It discusses:

- Carroll’s CSR framework;
- corporate social irresponsibility;
- public scepticism about symbolic CSR;
- CSR as a “social licence to operate”;
- purpose, values, legitimacy, and competitive differentiation;
- the particular resource constraints faced by SMEs.

### Creating shared and integrated value

Porter and Kramer’s **Creating Shared Value** links social outcomes with competitive advantage and profitability. Visser’s **Integrated Value Creation** extends this toward innovation and transformation in response to systemic global problems.

The proposal adopts a pragmatic position: profitability remains necessary, but CSR and social value increasingly influence customer relationships, legitimacy, and long-term value creation.

### Cross-sector collaborative partnerships

Dialogue-based partnerships are proposed as a way for SMEs and NPOs to combine complementary strengths. The literature also identifies risks:

- resource and power imbalances;
- reputational exposure;
- incompatible cultures and objectives;
- shallow, philanthropic relationships;
- unclear outcomes;
- weak leadership commitment.

### Collaborative Value Creation framework

Austin and Seitanidi’s framework provides the main analytical foundation:

1. **Value-creation spectrum:** complementary resources, interests, value direction, and use.
2. **Collaboration stages:** movement from philanthropic to transactional, integrative, and transformational relationships.
3. **Partnership processes:** how interaction and governance produce value.
4. **Collaborative outcomes:** effects on individuals, organisations, and society.

### Impact and value measurement

The proposal compares business tools such as the Balanced Scorecard with NPO tools such as theory of change and logic models. It argues that credible partnership value requires evidence of outcomes and impact, not only outputs or anecdotal CSR reporting.

## Expected contribution and outputs

This proposal presents no completed interviews, findings, or tested maturity framework. The intended outputs are:

- a richer understanding of why Canadian SME leaders enter NPO partnerships;
- evidence about how they perceive shared value;
- criteria for progressing from philanthropic toward transformational collaboration;
- guidance on partnership design, leadership commitment, culture, resources, and measurement;
- a practical maturity framework for the focal NPO and prospective SME partners.

## Methodological strengths

- The proposal addresses a clearly stated practical problem through an established theoretical framework.
- Interviewing CEOs targets people who can authorise CSR partnerships.
- The planned maturity framework creates a tangible applied output.
- Measurement is treated as integral to partnership value rather than an afterthought.
- Reflexivity, participant validation, and comparison with literature strengthen the qualitative design.

## Limitations and points to watch

- Ten purposively selected CEOs cannot represent Canadian SMEs broadly.
- The design appears one-sided: it studies SME leaders while asking how two kinds of organisations collaborate.
- Cross-sectional perceptions cannot show how partnerships mature over time.
- Context during the upheaval of 2020 may shape responses unusually strongly.
- The proposal risks embedding a preferred conclusion—that deeper collaboration is desirable—before data collection.
- Body-language observations may be difficult to interpret consistently, particularly if interviews are remote.
- A maturity framework derived from a small sample would need validation with NPOs and actual partnerships.

## Why read this proposal

Read this if you are considering an applied dissertation that moves from literature to a practical framework. It is strongest in its synthesis of CSR, shared value, cross-sector collaboration, and impact measurement. It also provides useful material for critically assessing whether participant selection truly represents every side of a partnership.
