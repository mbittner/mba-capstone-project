## Main content

### From proposal to dissertation

The high-level process is:

1. create a strong proposal;
2. carry out well-designed, ethically approved research;
3. follow the current Module Handbook;
4. write the dissertation chapter by chapter;
5. obtain feedback throughout; and
6. leave time for whole-document revision.

The Module Handbook is presented as the authoritative guide to content and marking. Its criteria should be used as a planning checklist and self-assessment rubric, not consulted only at submission.

### Proposal structure

A strong proposal contains:

- **Introduction/problem:** background, relevance, a focused research question, and clear objectives.
- **Literature review:** selective but sufficiently broad and deep, critically analysed, correctly referenced, logically developed, and directly connected to the project.
- **Methodology:** evidence required, sample and target size, collection tool, advantages over alternatives, analysis plan, and methods references.
- **Limitations and mitigation:** a concise, realistic risk table.
- **Project plan:** milestones shown as a Gantt/bar chart or equivalent.

The proposal should demonstrate that the project is valuable, feasible, methodologically defensible, and capable of answering the question.

### Dissertation structure

The slides describe the following components:

#### Abstract

A one-page summary of the purpose, key literature, method/sample, findings, and conclusions. It is written most effectively after the dissertation is complete.

#### Introduction

Sets out the problem, question, objectives, significance, method, and document structure. Avoid unsupported claims and casual assertions that there is a “gap in the literature.”

#### Literature review

Critically evaluates the relevant literature, develops the conceptual basis for the study, informs the research design, and provides theories used later to interpret findings. It should end with a synthesis of the key points.

#### Methodology

Explains research philosophy and strategy, evidence gathered, sample, collection methods, analytical procedure, ethics, limitations, quality, and mitigation. Method choices must be supported by research-methods literature.

#### Findings

Presents results logically and credibly. Quantitative evidence may use justified statistics and tables/charts. Qualitative evidence should present themes supported by quotations or extracts. The relevance of the results should be explained and summarised.

#### Discussion/analysis

Interprets the principal findings through the literature, identifies agreement or disagreement with earlier studies or theory, offers reasoned explanations, and returns to the research question.

#### Conclusions and recommendations

Answers the question and objectives, follows from the findings and analysis, makes contextually realistic recommendations, and introduces no new evidence.

#### Reflection

Critically evaluates the dissertation process, identifies skills applied and developed, examines difficulties and decisions, and establishes future development needs.

The repeated visual message is that these sections must form one integrated line of reasoning.

### Research-strategy review

The virtual-team exercise review concludes that:

- 12 semi-structured interviews can provide rich experiences and allow probing, but cannot support claims about the whole bank;
- a focused qualitative case in one function or team level may be stronger than superficial organisational breadth;
- a large questionnaire can provide broader and potentially more representative evidence when prior literature supports meaningful standard categories;
- questionnaires limit answers to the researcher's categories and do not permit clarification or probing; and
- combining both methods substantially increases work.

The design must match the final objective. A recommendation applying to the whole organisation may require broader evidence; a small qualitative study should make bounded recommendations or recommend further research.

### Reflective practice

Reflection is presented as systematic learning from experience, not simply remembering what happened. The materials distinguish:

- **reflection-on-action:** retrospective examination;
- **reflection-in-action:** noticing and adapting during the activity; and
- **reflection-for-action:** converting learning into future intention.

One reflection-on-action sequence is:

> Re-inhabit → Reflect → Review → Reframe

Most models combine retrospection, critical self-evaluation, and reorientation toward future practice.

The Gibbs-style prompts cover:

- description;
- feelings and perspectives;
- evaluation of what worked and did not;
- analysis of assumptions and new insight;
- conclusions; and
- a specific action plan.

Suggested practices include short regular journal entries, free writing, visual mapping, reflective dialogue, and narrative reflection. Capturing observations throughout the dissertation is more credible and useful than reconstructing the whole experience at the end.

### Reflective academic writing

Good reflective writing:

- briefly identifies a significant incident or decision;
- examines thinking, feelings, assumptions, and other perspectives;
- uses evidence to evaluate outcomes;
- connects experience to relevant academic literature;
- explores agreement or tension between theory and experience;
- identifies learning; and
- ends with a justified, specific action plan.

Common weaknesses are excessive narrative, unsupported claims about success, little academic literature, and no clear future action.

### Development form and self-assessment

The development form asks for:

- broad interest and specific issue;
- access to people, documents, or other evidence;
- information that can realistically be gathered and how;
- relevant literature; and
- draft question and objectives.

The self-assessment tests historical module facts and good-practice guidance. Its answer key is in Friday's folder. Because some answers concern 2021–2022 rules, they should be used for learning but checked against the current handbook.

## Practical next steps

- Turn the development form into a one-page concept note.
- Prepare a short oral structure: problem, question, objectives, literature, evidence access, method, feasibility, ethics, and main uncertainty.
- Build a limitations/mitigation register and project schedule.
- Create a chapter-alignment matrix linking each objective to literature, evidence, analysis, and conclusion.
- Agree draft-feedback milestones with the supervisor early.
- Record major methodological decisions and why they were made for the eventual reflection chapter.
- Use feedback from Friday to revise the question and design, not merely to confirm the initial idea.
