# Leadership styles and organisational change

**Dissertation:** *The relationship between leadership styles and a change process in a third party processor: A study of Processing International*  
**Author:** Reda Mohamed Helal (2012)  
**Best for:** Examining perceived leadership style, organisational change, employee satisfaction, and intention to stay through structured questionnaires.

## Research question

The dissertation does not state one concise interrogative question. Its title and stated aim function as the question: what relationship exists between leadership styles and the change process at the pseudonymous organisation “Processing International” (PI), and which style is most effective during change?

## Objectives

The study aims to:

1. Review theories of leadership, organisational change, and their interconnection.
2. Examine the relationship between leadership styles and the change process at PI.
3. Determine the leadership style preferred for effective change.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

This is a cross-sectional case study of PI operations in the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt.

- Leaders completed a 30-item leadership self-assessment that classified their approach as autocratic, participative, or delegative.
- Eleven leaders appear to have participated, although one part of the dissertation refers to ten.
- Seventy-six employees completed a researcher-developed questionnaire about their manager’s leadership style, the change process, job satisfaction, and intention to remain.
- The subordinate questionnaire was piloted for two weeks.
- Responses were scored and compared in Microsoft Excel, including comparisons across departments and leaders.

The study uses an interpretive discussion of a structured, predominantly quantitative dataset. It assesses relationships among perceptions; it does not observe leader behaviour or measure causal effects on performance.

### Key literature and concepts

The literature review centres on:

- **Leadership traits and definitions:** leadership as influence rather than position alone.
- **Three leadership styles:** autocratic, participative, and delegative leadership, drawing particularly on Lewin and later leadership-continuum work.
- **Strategic leadership:** the leader’s role in direction, alignment, and organisational performance.
- **Change leadership and change agents:** communication, participation, resistance, culture, and the behaviours needed to implement change.
- **Organisational culture:** how local and company norms influence both leadership and employee reactions.
- **Commitment and satisfaction:** relationships among leadership style, employee morale, loyalty, and intention to leave.
- **Regional and cultural context:** the possibility that findings from Western leadership research will not transfer directly to a culturally diverse Middle Eastern workplace.

## Main findings

- Participative leadership was the most common style at PI, with delegative leadership second.
- Departments led in a more autocratic manner showed more negative perceptions of organisational change.
- A combination of participative and delegative behaviour was associated with more positive change perceptions and stronger satisfaction or loyalty than participative leadership alone.
- Participative leadership by itself did not guarantee employee satisfaction. Some highly participative departments, including Projects and IT, still had high intention to leave.
- Across the organisation, satisfaction and retention signals were poor: only around one-fifth of respondents were not thinking about leaving, fewer than one-third would recommend PI, and approximately one-quarter reported satisfaction.
- Employee reactions were influenced by more than leadership style. Culture, communication, local labour-market opportunities, and personal circumstances also mattered.

The findings therefore challenge a simple universal claim that participative leadership automatically produces satisfaction and effective change.

## Conclusion and recommendations

The author concludes that autocratic leadership is generally less suitable for the change context studied. Participative leadership is useful, but is more effective when combined with appropriate delegation, information sharing, and employee involvement.

Recommendations include:

- analyse leadership behaviour more broadly than a three-style classification;
- provide employees with clearer and more regular information about the change;
- increase transparency around appointments and external hires;
- strengthen internal promotion and development opportunities;
- provide leadership training;
- create a representative employee or change-liaison group to improve two-way communication.

## Limitations and cautions

- The study covers one company during an ongoing restructuring.
- The sample is small, and the leader count is reported inconsistently.
- Both leadership and change are measured through self-report and employee perception.
- Fear, social desirability, or job insecurity may have affected responses.
- The cross-sectional design cannot show that leadership style caused satisfaction, loyalty, or change outcomes.
- National, organisational, and departmental cultures make generalisation difficult.

## Why read this dissertation

Read this if you want an example of a practical leadership-style questionnaire paired with employee perceptions. It is useful for seeing how simple quantitative scoring can support an applied case study, but it is also a good reminder to avoid overstating causality from cross-sectional self-report data.
