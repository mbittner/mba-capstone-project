# Monday — Summary

## Overview

Monday introduces the residency, establishes the week's learning process, and concentrates on two foundations of the dissertation:

1. finding credible academic literature; and
2. turning a broad interest into one focused research question with a small set of aligned objectives.

The folder contains six presentation files and four study documents. Several presentations are duplicate narrated/non-narrated versions. The substantive material dates mainly from 2021–2022, so current library links and module rules should be verified.

## Main content

### Residency purpose and working method

The residency aims to clarify what the dissertation requires and build or reinforce the research skills needed to complete it. The format combines short tutor inputs, questions, small-group work, exercises between sessions, and review discussions.

The stated ground rules are respect, active learning, confidentiality, and appropriate handling of recording. Monday prepares later sessions on qualitative and quantitative research, research strategies, dissertation writing, and the Friday project discussion.

### Literature searching

Dissertation research normally requires literature beyond the theories used in earlier taught modules. Sources should be specific to the research question.

The suggested source hierarchy is:

- peer-reviewed journal articles as the strongest default;
- academic books written by subject specialists;
- reputable reports from major consultancies where relevant; and
- other web material only after careful credibility assessment.

The YSJ library databases and Google Scholar are both recommended. Exercise 1 requires five relevant papers to be found, downloaded, and saved, with notes on successful search techniques and any difficulties.

The practical learning objective is not merely to collect five articles. It is to begin developing a reproducible search process: identify concepts, generate synonyms, test combinations, assess relevance and quality, and record what worked.

### Research question and objectives

The research question and objectives “set the mission, vision and direction” for the project.

- The **research question** states what the project will find out.
- The **research objectives** identify the work needed to answer the question.
- The slides recommend **one focused question and three or four objectives**.
- The first objective should normally state the literature to be critically reviewed.
- The remaining objectives should state what evidence will be gathered and how it will be analysed.

The recommended progression is:

> Broad area → particular focus → clear research question → specific objectives

Good questions in the slides are bounded by phenomenon and context, such as leadership competencies in a specific humanitarian setting or resistance to change in a particular case-study organisation. The central lesson is that narrowing the subject requires deliberate choices about what will and will not be studied.

Exercise 2 illustrates common problems:

- too many overlapping aims and objectives;
- a question that is not precisely worded;
- objectives that cannot realistically be evidenced;
- methods being placed into objectives without clear analytical purpose; and
- later objectives losing alignment with the concepts named in the question.

The examples should be assessed for clarity, specificity, feasibility, and internal alignment.

### Survey-design exercise

Exercise 3 presents a questionnaire about cross-functional teams and value creation. It is deliberately used as material for critique rather than as a model to copy.

The exercise asks:

- Will the responses actually answer the stated research questions?
- Are the questions clear and unambiguous?
- Are all respondents answering about the same type of team?
- Are key concepts such as “cross-functional team” and “diversity” defined?
- Are response categories complete, mutually exclusive, and inclusive?
- Does each question provide information that will be used in the analysis?
- Are demographic questions necessary and appropriately placed?

The questionnaire has some strengths: it is relatively short, divided into sections, mainly uses consistent response scales, connects its sections to a conceptual framework, and includes an open comment field. Its weaknesses are addressed in Tuesday's review material.

### Qualitative-data analysis

The qualitative-analysis paper is preparatory reading for the later coding exercise. It identifies interviews, open survey responses, statements, and organisational documents as possible qualitative data.

Three broad analytical approaches are described:

- **Deductive:** apply categories taken from an existing academic theory.
- **Inductive/grounded:** develop codes and themes from the data, then relate the results to published theory.
- **Hybrid:** use theory-informed categories while remaining open to findings the framework does not explain.

The paper follows Braun and Clarke's broad sequence:

1. become familiar with the data;
2. generate initial codes;
3. search for themes;
4. review themes against extracts and the full dataset;
5. define and name themes; and
6. select evidence, relate the analysis to the question and literature, and write the report.

Coding requires interpretation, iteration, and an audit trail linking each code or theme to the source evidence. Findings should include representative quotations or extracts so readers can assess whether the interpretation is credible. With small interview samples, an uncommon but analytically important insight may matter as much as frequency.

Different authors use “code,” “category,” and “theme” differently. The guidance is to select terminology from an appropriate published source, explain it, and use it consistently.

## Monday deliverables

1. Identify a provisional dissertation topic.
2. Search the current YSJ databases and locate five relevant, downloadable academic papers.
3. Record databases, search terms, filters, useful techniques, and problems encountered.
4. Draft one focused research question.
5. Draft three or four objectives, beginning with a critical-literature objective.
6. Check that every objective contributes directly to answering the question.
7. Critique the two research-question examples in Exercise 2.
8. Review the survey in Exercise 3 and note specific improvements.
9. Read the qualitative-analysis overview before the coding exercise later in the week.

## Practical next steps

- Create a literature-search table with the concepts in the draft question, synonyms, exact phrases, and alternative spellings.
- Start a literature matrix recording citation, theory, context, method, sample, findings, limitations, and relevance.
- Test the question against available participants, organisational access, ethics, time, and word limit.
- Rewrite the question and objectives after the first literature search.
- Avoid committing to a data-collection method until the question, evidence needed, access, and methodological trade-offs are clearer.
- Save all early ideas and search records; they can support the proposal's literature and methodology sections.

## Important cautions

- The presentations and exercises are dated 2021–2022. Current library interfaces, links, module guidance, and ethics procedures may differ.
- Narrated and non-narrated presentations are duplicates in substantive slide content.
- Example questions, sample sizes, codes, and survey items are teaching illustrations, not approved templates.
- No participant data should be collected during exploratory planning unless current ethics approval explicitly permits it.
