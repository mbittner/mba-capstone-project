# Effective leadership in a health service

**Dissertation:** *Effective Leadership in a Health Service: A case study of a healthcare organisation that has recently undergone structural change*  
**Author:** Alistair Squires (2014)  
**Best for:** A sequential mixed-method design that develops an organisation-specific definition through interviews and then tests it with a survey.

## Research question

> What makes leadership effective within a Health Service?

The study also asks whether general leadership principles apply unchanged in healthcare and whether the desired leadership is evident in the case organisation.

## Objectives

The dissertation aims to:

1. Understand what literature says about effective leadership generally and in healthcare.
2. Establish the meaning of effective leadership within “AnHHS.”
3. Determine whether effective leadership exists there, measured against theoretical and internally derived definitions.
4. Recommend improvement strategies.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

This is an exploratory, inductive, sequential mixed-method case study guided by interpretivism.

The case is a geographically dispersed Queensland public health service with about 4,700 staff, 26 facilities, and a recently devolved governance structure.

**Stage one—interviews**

- Ten leaders were purposively selected: five of ten board directors and five of seven executives.
- The sample sought variety in clinical training, active clinical practice, non-health experience, skills, and background.
- Semi-structured interviews were recorded and professionally transcribed.
- First-cycle content coding identified repeated words and grouped them as attributes, skills/knowledge, and behaviours.
- Further cycles identified broader concepts and illustrative quotations.

**Stage two—questionnaire**

- Interview themes and literature informed an online questionnaire.
- Approximately 350 managers and leaders were invited.
- 164 usable responses were received, a 46.9% response rate.
- Respondents defined what leadership **should be**, assessed what it **currently is**, and evaluated themselves.
- Quantitative comparisons included clinician/non-clinician perspectives.

The researcher was the organisation’s head of internal audit, providing unusual access but also creating possible authority and interpretation bias.

### Key literature and concepts

The literature review includes:

- **Leadership versus management:** direction, alignment, and motivation compared with planning, control, and operational delivery.
- **Evolution of leadership theory:** traits, learned skills, behaviour, contingency, and situational approaches.
- **Full Range Leadership Theory:** transformational, transactional, management-by-exception, and laissez-faire leadership.
- **Emotional intelligence:** self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, and relationship skills; Goleman’s coercive, authoritative, affiliative, democratic, pacesetting, and coaching styles.
- **Healthcare leadership levels:** board, executive, clinical, and functional leadership.
- **NHS Leadership Qualities Framework:** personal qualities, setting direction, and delivering service.
- **Teams and learning organisations:** team development, shared vision, systems thinking, and learning at individual, network, and organisational levels.
- **Level 5 leadership:** humility, professional will, disciplined people, and the right people in the right roles.
- **Exemplary leadership practices:** model the way, inspire a shared vision, challenge the process, enable others, and encourage the heart.
- **Social-sector context:** mission, complex stakeholders, professional autonomy, public accountability, and non-financial measures of success.

The review found few qualities unique to healthcare; the context changes how leadership is exercised more than the basic qualities required.

## Main findings

Clinicians and non-clinicians largely agreed on the desired definition of effective leadership. Effective leaders:

- **are** honest, self-aware, diligent, decisive, passionate, and driven;
- **have** a lifelong-learning mindset, expertise, cultural sensitivity, strategic-thinking ability, and sector knowledge;
- **behave** by leading through example, treating others respectfully, communicating clearly and frequently, building teams, and listening actively.

However, respondents saw a substantial gap between importance and actual practice. For the top qualities:

- desired attributes averaged 96.6%, while perceived existence averaged 61.5%;
- desired skills and knowledge averaged 92.5%, while existence averaged 53.4%;
- desired behaviours averaged 99.2%, while existence averaged only 36.8%.

Communication, team building, listening, respect, strategic thinking, self-awareness, and leading by example showed particularly large gaps.

Other findings:

- Clinicians evaluated existing leadership more positively than non-clinicians, especially its behaviour.
- Interviewed executives and directors called leadership effective because the organisation had balanced budgets, reduced waiting lists, maintained safety, and met targets.
- Those measures resemble management outcomes more than the relationship and inspiration qualities used to define leadership.
- The early coercive or authoritative style may have been appropriate immediately after structural change, but its effectiveness was fading.
- Respondents rated themselves more positively than they rated leadership overall.

## Conclusion and recommendations

Through the lens of short-term goal achievement, leadership had been effective. Through the organisation’s own desired definition, effective leadership did not currently exist consistently.

The author recommends:

1. Develop an internal leadership model and train leaders to recognise and shift among styles.
2. Conduct a broader staff survey using different organisational lenses.
3. Perform a strengths audit and align roles with strengths where possible.
4. Use self- and peer-evaluation, supported by established leadership tools.
5. Build regular team-development, open discussion, improvement planning, and celebration into leadership practice.

## Limitations and cautions

- Fewer than 400 people contributed from a workforce of about 4,700.
- The survey targeted managers and leaders, not the wider follower population.
- Only interviews and a survey were used; longitudinal observation could have compared reported and actual behaviour.
- Insider status and the author’s audit role may have affected participation and interpretation.
- Findings should not be generalised beyond the organisation or, at most, similar Queensland health services.

## Why read this dissertation

Read this for a strong example of sequential mixed methods: qualitative interviews generate a local framework, and a survey then tests its importance and perceived existence. It is especially relevant to healthcare, public services, post-restructure leadership, and desired-versus-actual gap analysis.
