# Innovation practices in Cambodian microfinance institutions

**Dissertation:** *Exploring innovation practices amongst licensed microfinance institutions in Cambodia*  
**Author:** Marcela Binova (2013)  
**Best for:** A sector-level study of how firms in a developing economy turn pressure to innovate into internal practices.

## Research question

> How do Cambodian MFIs translate the environmental pressures and aspirations to innovate into their internal processes and behaviours?

## Objectives

The research aims to:

1. Review firm-level innovation literature in developing countries, Cambodia, and microfinance.
2. Identify which types of innovation Cambodian microfinance institutions consider most important and which they have actually adopted.
3. Explore internal processes and behaviours used to generate and manage ideas.
4. Identify the principal motivations for, and obstacles to, innovation.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

The study is descriptive and exploratory, cross-sectional, and based on ten embedded organisational cases from Cambodia’s 32 licensed microfinance institutions.

- Ten C-level executives from institutions of different sizes participated.
- Data was gathered through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews.
- Participants received a questionnaire in advance. Interviews were conducted in English, recorded, and transcribed.
- Quantitative elements included rankings, frequencies, weighted ordinal counts, and limited tests comparing demographic characteristics.
- Qualitative material was coded to identify themes and explanations behind the rankings.
- A pilot interview was conducted with an executive outside the sector.
- Results were triangulated across interview narratives, quantitative rankings, and the literature.

The design combines post-positivist measurement with interpretive inquiry. It is a qualitative-dominant mixed approach rather than a statistically representative sector survey.

### Key literature and concepts

The literature review covers:

- **Definitions of innovation:** distinctions among innovation, creativity, invention, imitation, and adoption.
- **Innovation types:** product, service, process, delivery-channel, marketing, customer-facing, strategic, organisational, incremental, and radical innovation.
- **Innovation models and generations:** movement from linear internal models toward interactive, networked, and open innovation.
- **Innovation in developing economies:** informal innovation, imitation and catching up, limited infrastructure, institutional constraints, and the applicability of Western innovation models.
- **Absorptive capacity and collaboration:** the ability to find, absorb, adapt, and apply external knowledge.
- **Microfinance’s dual mission:** financial viability alongside poverty reduction and social impact.
- **Cambodian context:** regulation, rapid sector growth, competition, access to finance and technology, skills, and customer literacy.
- **Culture and feedback:** power distance, collectivism, and the difficulty of obtaining candid employee and customer input.

## Main findings

- All participants considered innovation important, often very or extremely important.
- Product innovation was both the highest priority and the most widely implemented category.
- Strategy, customer-facing processes, and delivery channels also ranked highly, but actual implementation did not always match stated priority. Marketing was among the most frequently implemented areas.
- Institutions communicated the importance of innovation, sought employee and customer feedback, collaborated externally, and frequently learned by imitating or adapting ideas already used elsewhere.
- Customer and staff feedback processes existed but were constrained by hierarchy, cultural reluctance to challenge authority, and customer literacy. Call centres had mixed effectiveness.
- External collaboration concentrated on consultants, investors, and donors. Other possible partners and wider knowledge networks were underused.
- Idea management was usually decentralised and informal. Some institutions used office tools or designated staff, but few had a mature, systematic innovation process.
- The strongest motivation was customer satisfaction, followed by product expansion, quality improvement, and operating efficiency.
- The most important barriers were lack of information about technology, insufficient funding, shortages of skilled staff, regulation, and limited customer responsiveness.

The study suggests—but cannot establish statistically—that institution size, funding, and product breadth may influence innovation capacity.

## Conclusion and recommendations

Cambodian microfinance institutions were actively innovating, but their internal systems were more informal than their stated commitment to innovation might imply. Innovation commonly emerged through customer needs, employee ideas, collaboration, and adaptation of practices seen elsewhere.

The dissertation recommends further work on:

- the relationship between institution size and innovation capability;
- gaps between innovation priorities and implementation;
- more effective ways to obtain employee and low-literacy customer feedback;
- the effect of regulation and funding;
- broader external collaboration and knowledge sourcing;
- more systematic processes for recording, evaluating, and progressing ideas.

## Limitations and cautions

- Ten institutions are too few for reliable sector-wide or size-based statistical claims.
- The research intentionally covers a wide range of innovation topics, limiting depth in any single area.
- C-level participants may present their institutions more positively than employees or customers would.
- Conducting interviews in English may have reduced nuance.
- Cultural interpretation and the researcher’s sector access may have influenced the evidence.

## Why read this dissertation

Read this for a strong example of a qualitative-dominant mixed design, executive interviews across several organisations, or research on innovation in an emerging economy. The priority-versus-implementation comparison is especially useful as a model for structuring an applied dissertation.
