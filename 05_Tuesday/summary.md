## Main content

### Research-question and objective review

The review recommends using the research question as the overall aim and avoiding a separate layer of “aims.” A focused project normally has one research question and three or four objectives.

The first objective should identify the literature to be critically reviewed. Later objectives should gather and analyse evidence in direct alignment with the concepts and context named in the question.

Research questions and objectives may evolve, but material changes should be discussed with the supervisor. The review stresses that too many overlapping objectives increase the risk that parts of the project will not be completed or answered.

### Questionnaire design

Questionnaires are appropriate when standardised information is needed from many respondents, the topic is sufficiently understood to formulate meaningful answer categories, and respondents can understand the subject and questions.

Before writing questions, define:

- **What and why:** the questionnaire's purpose and the evidence needed for the research question.
- **Who:** the intended respondents and the language appropriate to them.
- **How and when:** distribution channel, access, timing, and closure plan.

Good questionnaire design requires:

- a clear link between every question and the research question/objectives;
- literature-based justification for concepts and measures where possible;
- clear respondent instructions;
- simple, unambiguous wording;
- logical sequencing and clean layout;
- appropriate and non-overlapping response categories;
- an introduction or covering statement;
- supervisor review; and
- pre-testing/piloting followed by revision.

Question types include closed categories, Likert-style agreement statements, select-all-that-apply lists, rankings, forced choices, and open questions. Open questions may reveal unexpected information but increase respondent effort and create qualitative analysis work. An “Other—please specify” option can reduce the risk of incomplete answer categories.

Common design risks include ambiguity, leading language, double-barrelled questions, unwarranted assumptions, offensive or confronting wording, incomplete categories, overlapping ranges, undefined concepts, and collecting demographics that have no analytical use.

### Review of the sample survey

The survey is short, sectioned, mainly clear, linked to a conceptual framework, and includes an open question. However, the review identifies important validity problems:

- It is unclear whether the questions can answer the three stated research questions.
- Respondents may belong to multiple cross-functional teams with different experiences.
- “Cross-functional team” is not defined.
- Some sections may prompt answers about ordinary departmental teams instead.
- Demographics appear at the beginning rather than the end.
- Gender options are not inclusive and omit “prefer not to say.”
- Service-length ranges overlap.
- Some questions are unclear, weakly defined, or of doubtful analytical value.

The core lesson is that a polished-looking survey is not valid unless the resulting data can answer the research question.

### Introductory quantitative analysis

The decks introduce:

- **Mean:** arithmetic average, which may be distorted by outliers.
- **Median:** middle value, often more representative for skewed data.
- **Mode:** most common value.
- **Chi-square:** tests whether differences between categorical groups may be statistically significant.
- **Correlation:** evaluates the strength and direction of association between variables.

The salary example shows why the mean can misrepresent a typical case when a few values are much larger than the rest. Selection of a statistic should depend on the measurement scale, distribution, sample, assumptions, and research question.

The material is introductory. A project using inferential statistics needs appropriate methodological support, correct assumption checks, transparent reporting, and preferably a supervisor comfortable with quantitative work.

### Interviews and qualitative data collection

Semi-structured interviews use a small set of core or “stem” questions while allowing follow-up probes. The decks suggest approximately 8–15 participants as a common dissertation-scale example, not an automatic requirement.

Good practice includes:

- use language the participant understands;
- ask open questions;
- keep the main-question list short;
- create rapport and ease the participant into the interview;
- listen more than speaking;
- probe examples, meanings, and significant details;
- avoid leading, multiple, confusing, or disguised questions;
- obtain supervisor feedback;
- pilot the interview;
- record where appropriate, with consent; and
- reflect after each interview and improve the process.

Questions about concrete work, priorities, behaviours, relationships, examples, advice, and learning may elicit richer data than asking participants to label their own leadership style.

Recording improves accuracy but creates consent, storage, security, transcription, and deletion responsibilities. The research plan must explain how the interview evidence will be captured and analysed.

### Coding exercise and thematic analysis

The Dorothy Edwards case asks the student to identify leadership behaviours, skills, and attitudes in an interview extract. Possible theory-informed themes include:

- communicating organisational purpose and vision;
- inspirational motivation;
- encouraging investigation and creativity;
- participative decision-making;
- individualised consideration;
- staff learning and development;
- role modelling by sharing difficult work;
- empowerment with selective intervention; and
- maintaining a common client focus to resolve conflict.

The purpose is not to discover one official answer. Codes must be defensible, grounded in quotations, and related to relevant theory. The next day's review demonstrates links to transformational leadership and other frameworks.

### Online collection and Qualtrics

Interviews and focus groups can be conducted through Teams, Zoom, Skype, FaceTime, or similar tools, provided participants have access and are comfortable using them. The guidance recommends:

- pilot the technology;
- prepare a short participant guide;
- check audio, charging, connectivity, and recording;
- manage turn-taking in online focus groups;
- explicitly inform participants about recording; and
- obtain consent.

Qualtrics is presented as the University's survey platform. The material explains question types, answer options, blocks, display and skip logic, previewing on desktop/mobile, publication of changes, expiry dates, partial responses, end messages, collaboration, distribution, and export/analysis.

The provided login and guidance date from 2019–2022 and should be verified before use.

## Practical next steps

- Build an evidence map linking every objective to data required, source/sample, collection method, and analysis.
- For interviews, draft 6–10 core open questions with planned probes.
- For questionnaires, create a construct-to-item table showing the literature or rationale behind each item.
- Pilot with people similar to the target participants and record all revisions.
- Decide how recordings, transcripts, survey exports, identifiers, and consent records will be stored securely.
- Consult the supervisor before committing to mixed methods or statistical tests.
- Confirm that the planned dataset will be large and suitable enough for the proposed quantitative analysis.

## Important cautions

- Sample-size figures are teaching recommendations, not universal rules.
- Correlation does not establish causation.
- Questionnaire software does not correct weak research design.
- Recording and online tools must be included in participant information, consent, and ethics materials.
- Do not collect participant data before written ethics approval.
- Verify current Qualtrics access, supported tools, and data-protection guidance.
