# Vision, leadership, and classroom technology

**Dissertation:** *The Impact of Vision and Leadership on Classroom Technology Deployments in UK Schools*  
**Author:** Peter Ormerod (2014)  
**Best for:** A large-scale quantitative survey, explicit research model, and analysis of direct and indirect leadership effects.

## Research question

The study tests the null hypothesis:

> Leadership and Vision are not significant factors in the effectiveness of classroom technology projects in the UK.

Its substantive aim is to determine whether vision and leadership style affect the educational, teacher, and school outcomes of technology deployment.

## Objectives

The dissertation aims to:

1. Review general and education-specific literature on leadership, vision, and classroom technology to identify variables.
2. Conduct a large quantitative study of technology projects in UK schools.
3. calculate effect-size differences among leadership styles so education leaders can use them in strategic planning.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

This is a cross-sectional, mono-method quantitative study with a predominantly deductive and broadly positivist/empiricist orientation.

- An online survey was distributed using a public database of more than 22,000 UK school email addresses.
- Five pilot groups of approximately 500 addresses were used to improve delivery, invitation wording, question length, timing, and reminders.
- The live survey produced 1,603 responses, a reported 7.8% response rate.
- Only fully completed questionnaires were analysed, leaving 1,059 cases.
- Respondents were asked to evaluate one substantial, school-wide classroom technology project.
- Most items used five-point Likert scales.
- Analysis used descriptive statistics, correlations, one-way ANOVA, aggregated variables, and effect-size comparisons.

The survey operationalised four leadership paradigms—transformational, managerial, instructional, and participative—and placed variables into a formal framework adapted from Leithwood and Levin.

### Key literature and conceptual framework

The review and model cover:

- **Educational technology:** expected gains in engagement, learning, inclusion, and teacher efficiency, alongside evidence of waste or weak impact.
- **Leadership definitions and models:** traits, behaviour, task/people orientations, distributed leadership, and educational leadership.
- **Transformational leadership:** vision, inspiration, culture, participation, teacher development, and change.
- **Managerial leadership:** planning, control, structure, and operational delivery.
- **Instructional leadership:** focus on teaching, curriculum, and learning.
- **Participative leadership:** shared decisions and distributed ownership.
- **Technology integration and change:** planning, training, implementation, barriers, and change management.
- **Vision:** local and national influences, clarity, responsibility, shared ownership, and proactive versus reactive motivation.

The research framework distinguishes:

- independent variables: leadership paradigm and vision;
- intermediate variables: technology competence, individual culture, and school culture;
- dependent variables: educational, teacher, and school outcomes;
- antecedent factors such as context, policy, school characteristics, and learner variables.

## Main findings

- A positive technology vision was common across UK schools, but responsibility for vision differed by leadership style.
- Managerial and instructional styles tended to place responsibility with leaders; transformational and participative styles were more likely to share it.
- Fear of falling behind was a stronger motivation than proactive creativity and innovation.
- Leadership style related to project outcomes mainly **indirectly**, through its influence on teacher and school culture, rather than only through implementation mechanics.
- Transformational leadership showed the most consistently positive relationships; managerial leadership was the weakest baseline.
- Compared with the managerial style, the transformational style was associated with approximately:
  - 24% stronger individual-teacher culture ratings;
  - 12% stronger school-culture ratings;
  - 12% stronger school-related outcomes;
  - 14% stronger teacher-related outcomes;
  - 8% stronger perceived educational outcomes.
- Respondents perceived school and teacher benefits more strongly than direct educational benefits to students.
- Technology competence and individual and school culture significantly influenced outcomes.

## Conclusion and implications

The null hypothesis is rejected in practical terms: leadership and vision matter. The most important mechanism is cultural—leaders shape teacher attitudes, school norms, motivation, and the environment in which technology is used.

The dissertation argues that:

- schools should develop transformational behaviours alongside competent project management;
- vision should be clear but shared;
- governors, head teachers, policy makers, and leadership-development bodies should understand the cultural effects of leadership;
- the UK emphasis on transformational leadership development is justified;
- actual academic indicators, such as assessment or inspection results, should be used in future research to validate perceived educational gains.

## Limitations and cautions

- Participation was voluntary, creating likely self-selection toward people interested in technology.
- School email addresses were not a random, stratified sample.
- Only complete responses were retained, which may introduce completion bias.
- Outcomes were perceptions rather than independent learning measures.
- The analysis simplified a large, interrelated variable set; a full multivariate model was beyond the study’s scope.
- Findings are UK-specific and should not automatically be transferred internationally.
- Reported percentage differences are survey-derived effect comparisons, not proof of causal gains.

## Why read this dissertation

Read this for the strongest quantitative design in the folder. The explicit variable model, extensive piloting, large sample, separation of direct and indirect effects, and transparent acknowledgment of analytical limits are especially useful.
