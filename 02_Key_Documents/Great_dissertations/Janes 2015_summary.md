# Exploring resistance to change

**Dissertation:** *Exploring resistance to change: Case study of a German sporting-goods company*  
**Author:** Steven Janes (2015)  
**Best for:** A rigorous qualitative case study of why organisational change meets resistance and how management responses can help or worsen it.

## Research question

> What caused resistance to the organisational change within the case study company, how did management respond, and how effective were their responses?

## Objectives

The dissertation aims to:

1. Critically review organisational change, resistance, and management interventions.
2. Interview stakeholders involved in the change.
3. Determine whether resistance occurred.
4. Identify its causes, or explain why the change did not proceed as planned.
5. identify and assess management’s responses.
6. Propose more effective interventions for future changes.

## How the dissertation was built

### Research design

This is a single, cross-sectional, qualitative case study conducted approximately ten months after a structural change in the IT function of a German sporting-goods company.

- Twelve stakeholders were purposively selected to provide contrasting perspectives: three senior-management-team members, five senior IT directors, three leaders of the new horizontal structure, and one consultant.
- Semi-structured interviews lasted roughly 30–50 minutes and were generally recorded and transcribed.
- Data was coded in MAXQDA. Categories were refined as analysis progressed and then compared in Excel.
- Secondary evidence included presentations, two Net Promoter Score surveys, force-field analysis, whiteboard material, and steering documents.
- The research is interpretivist, descriptive-explanatory, and mainly deductive: a framework derived from the literature was used to assess the case.

The researcher was an insider. Access and context were strengths, while possible bias and reluctance among participants were explicitly considered.

### Key literature and conceptual framework

The review treats resistance as more complex than irrational opposition:

- **Change typologies:** incremental versus discontinuous, continuous versus episodic, transformational versus realignment, and convergent versus radical change.
- **Meanings of resistance:** resistance can be overt or covert, behavioural or attitudinal, and may be legitimate feedback rather than a defect in employees.
- **Psychological transition:** recipients experience loss, uncertainty, and adjustment, not merely a new structure.
- **Communication and genuine participation:** early work by Coch and French and Lawrence underpins the distinction between real influence and symbolic consultation.
- **Stake and vested interest:** loss of status, authority, headcount, budget, expertise, or career value can shape attitudes.
- **Credibility and agreement:** employees may resist because they do not accept the proposed solution or trust those advancing it.
- **Intervention strategies:** participation, communication, negotiation, support, and—when necessary—clear direction.
- **Groupthink and emotional intelligence:** weak challenge at the decision stage can create poor solutions, while self-aware leaders are better able to interpret resistance.

The dissertation develops a practical framework around three main variables: agreement with the solution, genuine participation, and personal stake or vested interest.

## Main findings

- Ten of the twelve interviewees believed resistance occurred. The two exceptions illustrated that “resistance” itself was interpreted differently.
- Exclusion from meaningful participation was strongly associated with negative attitudes.
- Disagreement with the design and implementation of the new structure was a major cause.
- Perceived loss of status, budget, headcount, or authority increased resistance, although having a stake did not automatically produce opposition.
- Participation worked only when it provided actual influence. A change advisory body described as a place to complain, without decision power, became token participation and aggravated frustration.
- Some senior directors felt less able than others to voice concerns. People involved in the original decision tended to evaluate it more positively, consistent with a groupthink-like environment.
- Strong top-down direction from the CIO eventually ended overt resistance and was accepted by several participants. This suggests that clear directive leadership can be appropriate after consultation and deliberation have been exhausted.
- Some objections initially labelled as resistance were later validated. For example, a contested fee markup was subsequently removed.

The dissertation converts these findings into a model that predicts likely attitudes and behaviour from solution agreement, participation, and stake, moderated by contextual factors.

## Conclusion and recommendations

Resistance was not caused by one factor and should not be treated simply as obstruction. It reflected exclusion, disagreement, threatened interests, weak decision processes, and—in some cases—valid criticism.

The dissertation recommends:

- maximise genuine participation early enough to influence the solution;
- facilitate moderated debate and make disagreement safe;
- address expectations, fears, status concerns, and practical objections explicitly;
- listen respectfully and test the substance of resistance;
- distinguish useful challenge from behaviour that prevents implementation;
- when a final decision must stand, communicate it clearly and consistently without punishing earlier dissent.

## Limitations and cautions

- One organisation and one change limit generalisation.
- Accounts were retrospective and subjective.
- The insider researcher may have influenced access, disclosure, coding, and interpretation.
- The sample represents key stakeholders, not the wider employee population.
- The proposed model was developed from the case but not tested independently.

## Why read this dissertation

This is one of the strongest dissertations in the folder for qualitative case-study construction. Read it for its clear conceptual framework, purposive contrasting sample, multiple evidence sources, transparent coding, and nuanced treatment of resistance as both a risk and a potential source of better decisions.
